# Game Design Document

Working title: **Salt and Ember** (the sea and the flame: you and the lantern). The project, the game's window and the Mac app have been called that since #143; the repo is still called `restaurant`. In the story it's also the name of the ship that finds you at the end of the opening (the title drop, #231).

**How to read this document.** The game has a new world and a new opening, *the Unmooring* (#233; see "The world" and "The opening as intended"). Sections are marked **built** (it's in the game today), **planned** (decided, not built) or **open** (not decided yet). Much of what's below was written for the earlier opening (the rail talk, the rescue, the waking aboard with a captain beside you), and it's still what the build plays until the Unmooring replaces it, issue by issue (#227 to #232). Those sections say so where they start, and the Unmooring wins wherever the two disagree.

## Pitch
A cosy 3D adventure in the spirit of Ocarina of Time. Your island is held in place by a great chain, and you're its chain-tender, with a lantern spirit for a friend. The night the chain breaks, the sea sweeps you away from home and into the Grave of the Lanterns; the lantern goes in after you, and a dark ship finds you and takes you in. Home hasn't gone anywhere you can't follow: it has drifted off through the fog, as the islands are all doing now. You sail between them with a crew you gather, looking for it, and people find their way by an island's cooking and its songs, so you search by cooking and humming. Ashore, you explore, gather, solve puzzles and hunt wild creatures for the pot. Aboard, the ship's galley is the warm heart of it all, where the crew you gather eat together every evening.

## Pillars
1. **Wonder.** Like Kokiri Forest at the start of OoT, a real place where everything is new. Every island has new sights, sounds, tastes and textures, and there's always something round the next corner.
2. **Safe hearth, dangerous shore.** Once you're aboard, the ship is home: nothing ever attacks it. Danger, duels and puzzles live ashore (and out in the fog, before you reach her), so coming back aboard always feels like relief. She starts the game dark and abandoned (#231), and warming her is the first thing you do; how that goes is open.
3. **Food and music are the map.** Cooking and humming are how you search for home, how you prepare for adventure, and how you get to know the crew. People find their way by an island's cooking and its songs, so a dish or a tune is a kind of address.
4. **People are the heart.** The crew are the people from the islands that weren't home. Together they become home. (Open: who the first crew are, now the ship is empty when you find her.)
5. **Hands-on.** You walk your character between stations, across the deck and over the islands. The ship is a place, not a menu.

## The world (#233)
*Planned (decided; the opening's first piece, the rowing, is built: see "The Unmooring" under "The opening as intended").*
- **The chains.** The islands were once anchored by great chains running down into the sea; your island's rises from the water to the island, with a marker buoy out over it. The chains are breaking, and the islands drift away through the fog.
- **Finding your way.** People find their way by an island's cooking and its songs: a dish or a tune says where it's from. This is why food and music are the map (pillar 3).
- **Sea spirits.** Lanterns are one kind of sea spirit among many. Nobody ever told the lantern where lanterns go; the Grave of the Lanterns is the answer, and he finds it out the hard way (#230).
- **The Grave of the Lanterns** is where the sweep ends: a sea of dead lanterns that nobody you know has heard of, with great chains hanging down through it (already built, in the rescue). What it is, and what the thing beneath the chains is, is still to be written (see Open questions).

## Story
- **You:** your island's **chain-tender**. Every night you row out to the marker buoy over its chain, check it and keep its light lit, with the **lantern** (a lantern spirit, he/him) riding the bow pole: your friend, and the buoy's light (#227, #228). You aren't a castaway and you don't lose your memory: you know exactly where home is. It's just that home has drifted away. (The Player section still describes the castaway of the build as it stands.)
- **Your sibling's fish stew:** the taste of home is your **older sibling's fish stew**. They call from a lit window as you row out ("Back by supper. I'm doing the fish stew.") and you never see them. The stew is the dish you're cooking your way back to. (This replaces the earlier home dish, a bread with something bitter and green in it.)
- **The Unmooring (planned, #227 to #232):** the chain breaks while you're at the buoy, the sea sweeps you away from home and into the Grave of the Lanterns, the lantern goes in after you, and a dark ship called the Salt and Ember finds you in the fog. How it goes, beat by beat, is under "The opening as intended".
- **The rescue (built, to be rewritten):** you sink through the Grave of the Lanterns, a sea of dead lights. A small, warm light dives after you and a hand of flame takes yours. On the touch his light flows into you; he can't pull it back, and he gives you nearly all of it. In the build as it stands you wake aboard a ship and don't know him (the Prologue as first drafted: `docs/prologue.md`). In the Unmooring he's your friend: his lines in the water are rewritten for that (#230), and the rescue's pictures stay.
- **The promise:** the lantern promises to get you home. The first sketch had him sure you came from the nearest island, and cheerfully wrong, island after island ("perhaps your home *moved*?"). With the new premise it did: home really has drifted. Keep the cheerful guessing (he doesn't know where it's gone, and neither do you), and over time his guesses get more tender: he quietly hopes he's wrong, because if you find home, you'll leave the ship. (Open: how much of this stays, now he's known you all your life.) **He talks (first sketch):** a few words float beside him at the moments that matter: a fish landed ("A fish! Is home a fish? ...No. Probably not."), supper rung with nothing on the table, and waking at dawn. His lines are in `data/dialogue/moments.dlg`. **He has a home (first sketch, #93):** he lives on the little table at the foot of your bunk (since #206; before, the round table by the stove), and you go to him to talk (Interact by him). Supper is still shared at the round table, and he comes over to it (the remedy scene puts him there for now, until a dinner bell calls him). He no longer follows you about the ship. What he isn't there to see (a fish landed) he asks about when you next visit: "You smell of fish. Was it a good fish?" Near enough to see (about 8 m), he still says his line on the spot. His following (at your shoulder, hovering over what you stop at) is kept in the code for #65 to decide. **His warmth (first sketch):** he fades slowly over time (dimmer, his light reaching less far, never out), and says so when he's low ("I'm a bit... dim. Is there soup?"). Set a dish on the galley's long table and ring the bell there, and he drifts over to it, eats, and glows warm again, more for a better dish (see Supper, #207): the GDD's "a warm meal rekindles him", and a reason to cook well.
- **Tastes and songs, not lost memory:** you remember home perfectly well; what you have is its taste, your sibling's fish stew, and its songs. You search by cooking that stew and humming those tunes to the people you meet, who recognise flavours and melodies and point the way ("That's sea-fennel. That only grows in the north."). The dish gets closer as the voyage goes on. (The earlier version had you remember only a bread with something bitter and green in it and three notes of a song. The three-note hum is built (see Music); whether it stays as home's song is open.)
  - **Tastes of home:** a first sketch (a memory card popping up the first time you made or caught certain things) was taken out in #207: it read as a leftover. Tastes reminding you of home is still the idea, now as the fish stew and your sibling rather than a lost memory; it needs designing properly.
- **The crew:** each crew member comes from an island that turned out not to be home. The first was the gruff **heron captain**, and the second (in Chapter 1) is a **mossy rock cook**. The Unmooring puts you aboard an abandoned ship, so whether the captain is aboard when you arrive, and how the crew come to join, is open (see Open questions).
- **The ending question:** when you finally find home, do you stay?
- The wider story is still to be written.

## How it begins (first sketch)
*This section describes the opening as it's built today (**built**), and it's about to be replaced. The new opening is the Unmooring (#233; see "The opening as intended"): it starts at home on the water at dusk, not on the ship's nameboard, and the rail talk, the captain beside you and the waking aboard in this section go away or change. What carries over: the rescue's pictures (#230), the lantern's look, and the ship and its cooking. Until the Unmooring's pieces land (#227 to #232), the build still plays this; each task updates this section as it lands.*

The game opens on **her name**. The title screen is the Salt and Ember's nameboard at her stern, under the gallery windows, in the rain: her name chiselled deep into a plank. A lantern swings on a hook above it, half asleep. The cuts are lit from wherever he is: the wall of each letter facing him glows, the far wall is dark and throws its shadow across the floor of the cut, and it all swings round as he passes. The letters he's left fall back into shadow, with only a faint ember glow down in the grooves; lightning shows all of it at once. "Press any button". It's there on every launch (#114). The name and the prompt are set in Cinzel, drawn from Roman carved capitals. The name is in heavy capitals cut into the wood itself, with the plank's grain running across it. While the title is up the gallery windows are off, so his light is what you see.

That lantern is the lantern spirit, keeping watch. On a saved voyage he turns from the board, pleased to see you, and the button takes you on to "Day 3. Morning" and your bunk. On a new one he talks with the captain over the Grave of the Lanterns (#182), the Prologue's first scene:
- **The stern.** The swinging stops and the camera draws back from the board to the whole stern in the rain, with the captain up at the wheel. Far down in the black water, faint warm lights come up (the open sea's `grave` glow, the `@grave on` cue). He leans out on his hook: "There you are..." / "Captain? I think I've found something." Cut to the captain at the wheel: "Something, or another one of your little lights?" He lifts off his hook and bobs up beside the captain. "Captain. Come take a look." He leads the captain to the starboard rail, where they stand together (#222). The two of them look out at the lights together: "Look."
- **The Grave.** The camera goes past him, out over the water and down (the clip `camera_goes_over` carries the shot), and the shot cuts to a set of its own (`scenes/prototype/intro/grave.tscn`, brought up with `@stage grave`): looking down through dark, rain-ringed water at the dead lanterns hanging at every depth, empty and dark against murk lit warm near the top by his light and black below, with no bottom. The rain reaches the foreground and the swell rolls into close mist. Some are tipped over, some have lost their caps, some hang off three great chains that run down into the dark, and seven still glow faintly, guttering like dying stars (those are the hanging lantern model, `ember_lantern.tscn`). "There must be hundreds of them." / "Thousands, most likely." / "I wonder how long they've been down there." / "Longer than either of us." The camera turns to the brightest, further on: "Wait. That one..."
- **The ringing.** Cut back to the rail with `@stage off cut`. The captain asks "What do you hear?" and a bell rings far below (a Kenney bell, pitched down). "That's strange."
- **The thing.** Over the side and down again, wide: something far bigger than anything else passes beneath the lanterns, never lit, seen only by what it hides, and the lanterns above it rock in its wake. The lines come in their own time: "Back away from the rail." / "I think I saw—"
- **The hand.** The camera finds it among the dead lights, beside one that still glows: you, hanging upright, eyes shut, arms hanging loose. "..." / "Captain?" / "What is it?" / "There's somebody in the water."
- **Overboard.** Cut back to the captain. "What?" / "Somebody overboard!" The captain turns on him ("Don't you dare—"), and before he's finished the lantern is over the rail and gone.

From out on the water we watch him go: the storm sea, her starboard quarter, and one small point of light arcing out from her rail through the rain, its reflection running across the waves, until it hits the water: a tall jet of spray, a crown thrown outwards, mist, a ring of foam spreading across the swell, and his glow under the surface for a moment after he's gone. Her name is dark behind him, and the rescue begins (#100, #102, #119, #120; rebuilt as the Prologue's Scene 1 in #183):

1. **The dark.** The storm cuts to nothing as the water closes over, and we're straight under with him. Far above, muffled, a bell starts: somebody heard him.
2. **The light.** Wide: you, small, hanging upright and slumped under the storm's surface, a round window of dim sky with the dark sea mirrored all round it. A point of amber comes through it, a long way off, the first warm thing in the game, with a rope of lit bubbles streaming up off him. He drives down to you in hard pushes, still warm and bright: the dive costs him nothing yet. The little ember still hanging beside you ties the underwater wide shot to the discovery from above. His light finds dead lanterns hanging in the dark all round you both.
3. **He finds you.** "Hello?" / "Can you hear me?" You don't answer. He dips to look under your bowed head, hangs there for a moment and pulls back with a small overshoot. Throughout the rescue he faces you (#203: `KeepsFacing` turns him towards you on top of his clips), so his door opens towards you and his hand comes out of it. "Oh, you're in a bad way..."
4. **He tries.** He gets under you and heaves, twice, his flame bristling with it, and you rise a little and sink again, further: "Come on. Come on!" He looks up at the surface, so far above, and back at you. "Hold on. I've got you."
5. **His hand.** A flame can't reach through glass, so he opens the little door in his glass (it doesn't crack), and his flame pours out into the shape of a hand and holds it out, steady: "Here. Take my hand." The scene waits, with you still sinking, until you press Interact and take it ("Take his hand" shows, with the button). It's the only thing you do. You stay afloat as your arm lifts. Your head comes up towards him and your eyelids flutter open at the touch. His hand closes on yours, and light fans out from between them.
6. **It catches.** A strange note through the water. He freezes, and his flame flares brighter: "Oh..."
7. **His light goes into you.** Then gold runs out of his hand along your arm, a stream of small bright motes, and you begin to glow, a warm light growing in your chest. At the same time a thinner thread of his glow sinks away from him, down towards the dead lanterns far below, fading as it goes deep: they're drawing his light out of him too (#203). "The lanterns down there..." / "they're calling my light." He tries to pull away, jerking back on his arm of fire, and it holds; his flame shrinks and cools towards blue: "I can't..." / "I can't hold on to it."
8. **The Grave stirs.** All round, the dead lanterns flicker, faint embers sputtering in them, each to its own rhythm. The storm's rumble drops out under a low note. He looks down. From high above the two of you, looking down past you into the deep, lightning comes down through the water twice, and far below, beneath the chains that hang down through the lanterns, something enormous is passing: a blunt head, a long body bending slowly, seen only by what it hides.
9. **He chooses.** He looks back at you, small and blue now, nearly out: "I don't know who you are..." / "But I can't let you go." He lets the last of it go: a burst of gold from your joined hands fills the water.
10. **White.** The burst becomes white, and everything goes silent: no storm, no sea. The white holds.
11. **Black.** The white fades slowly to black, and stays black for a while. How you came aboard isn't shown: the boathook leaning by the galley door afterwards says it. No chapter card.
12. **Waking (#158, rewritten for the Prologue in #184; to be replaced by climbing aboard an abandoned ship, #231).** A slow fade in on the bunk room at night, the storm outside, warm and lamplit: you asleep on your back in your bunk, head on the pillow. He's beside you (#206): home on the little table at the foot of your bunk, small and blue, barely lit after giving you nearly all of his light. He tips towards you and watches for you to stir. The captain is standing by the head of your bunk, watching over you. Your eyes open on their own, and while you're still lying down (a shot of you in your bunk, the captain over you and the lantern in the foreground) the captain speaks first: "Ah. You're awake." / "Good. I was beginning to wonder whether we'd rescued you or merely inconvenienced a corpse." Then you're up, between them, and the lantern, weak but talking: "I told you they'd wake up!" / "You also told me you were perfectly capable of swimming." / "I am!" / "Clearly." "Oh! Hello!": he rises off his table towards you, his flame gutters, and he sinks back home. "Oh. That's not ideal." / "You've used nearly all your light." / "I know." / "You could have left them." / "I know." The captain asks what you remember (on a shot over his shoulder, so you see your own face as you face him), and your replies are things you do, each a single choice: *(Shake your head)*, *(Say nothing)* for your name, *(Say nothing)* for where you came from, *(Look down)* for how you ended up in the water. "I see." / "...you're fortunate he's as stubborn as he is." / "You're welcome." / "I wasn't thanking you." / "Come along. You should have a look around." / "And try not to fall overboard again." Then the lantern: "Don't mind him. He's always like that." / "Oh! And you left your book here. It's on the table, by me." And the ship is yours: the first quest (see Quests). The captain goes up on deck to the helm, and that's where you find him for your first proper conversation, which ends with him giving you the cooking pot (#186).
13. **The journal (#224).** Pick up the battered book on Lantern’s table. You hold it above your head, then Lantern asks if you were a cook. When the conversation ends, the journal opens to Voyage. Reopen it with J or View/Back to read Voyage and Recipes.

His lines in the water are in the dialogue box, with his name. Most wait to be read; "Come on. Come on!" and "Here. Take my hand." come in their own time, as he heaves and as his hand forms. Underwater the storm is a dull rumble, and the lightning above still flickers down through the surface. The water has a body: his light fills it and falls away with distance, far things lose their colour to the sea, the water near him shimmers with his heat, and dust in the water shows only where a light finds it. His hand is the same soft fire as his flame, poured into the shape of a hand. In the water you wear only your underwear, your clothes left off (`undressed` on the castaway's `CharacterModel`), and you hang far enough from him that taking his hand is a full stretch, your shoulder rising into it. While you're out cold you hang upright and slumped, eyes shut, legs trailing bent and drifting as a floating body's do rather than standing; taking his hand brings your head up and opens your eyes briefly. (A physics ragdoll was tried for the limp body and taken out: the limbs went everywhere.) The pieces: his light pouring into you is `Flow` in the underwater set (`LightFlow`: motes from his palm to your chest, and the glow in you); the flicker is `LanternField.flicker`; the chains are two `HangingChain`s; the white is `@fade white`. Some of its sounds are still to be recorded (his plunge, the bell, his hiss, the strange note at the touch, the low note under the thing): until then those moments are silent.

Hold Tab (Start on a controller) for a second to skip it. (In `sea.tscn`, the `Opening` node; tick its Skip to start on deck, or set its Start Section to open on a later beat. The script is `data/dialogue/intro.dlg`: see Cutscenes under Conversations. The underwater set is `scenes/prototype/intro/underwater.tscn`, the plunge beside the ship is in `rescue_set.tscn` next to it, and the nameboard's letters, the hook, the lantern on it and his clips are in `stern_watch.tscn`.)

**Keeping the voyage (first sketch):** sleeping in your bunk is where the game saves: the day and whether it's morning, and the lantern's warmth. Start again and the opening carries on ("Day 3. Morning", "You're back. I kept the light on.") instead of beginning anew. To start a new voyage, delete `voyage.save` from the user data folder.

### A Little Warmth (#224)

The captain stays at the bunk until you rise and says you both gave him a fright. The book Lantern kept for you is your journal. It opens once when picked up. J or View/Back reopens it during play. Voyage records what happened in your voice and shows the next step. Recipes gives the steps for each dish.

Find the captain at the wheel for the pot, then read the cookbook by the galley stove. The navigation room stays closed in the prologue. Fresh water is a small discovery. You can spot the captain drinking on deck, hear the rain hitting the barrel, or ask him directly. Filling the pot shows a short progress bar and updates the toolbar when the water goes in.

The pantry keeps a reserve of tomatoes, onions and chilli for retries. The slops bucket empties a batch and leaves the vessel in your hand. Ladle soup into a bowl, set the meal at the placemat, and ring the bell. The captain and Lantern come to the table for any meal. Plain tomato soup can go back in the pot with chilli. Spicy tomato soup restores Lantern's light, and he rises to show the captain.

## A day is a song
A day has four parts, like a song:
- **Morning ashore, the verse:** different every day. Explore an island, gather ingredients, solve its puzzles, duel what lives there, meet its people.
- **Afternoon sailing, the bridge:** the crew sails on through the fog. Wonders at scale drift past (towering kelp, a heron the size of a lighthouse, a sea arch carved like a face).
- **Evening in the galley, the chorus:** cook dinner, and the crew gathers at the galley table to eat, talk and play music. It comes back every day, growing richer as the crew grows.
- **Night on deck, the outro:** hum the memory melody into the fog, and hope something answers.

The ship's bell marks the day, as on real ships: eight bells calls everyone down to dinner.

## The ship
- A big wooden ship with a small crew. (In the Unmooring she starts dark and abandoned when she finds you, #231; how the crew come aboard is open.) A **crew member steers** by default. Taking the wheel yourself comes later.
- **Deck:** open to the sky, a working ship in a storm, built in three levels so it never reads as a flat platform (#90). Ship space: the bow is -z, the main deck's top is 5.3 m up.
  - **The shape (#196):** a real ship's hull made in Meshy, drawn as made: curved planking with two dark wales and a gilt capped top edge, sweeping up from the waist to a raised bow and a raised stern, a pointed stem carrying the gilded heron figurehead, a keel and a rudder. Its stern is a plain planked transom with a name board: no windows are painted on, so we choose where windows go. A long bowsprit runs out over the stem with a lantern hung from it, steeved up steeply enough (about 23 degrees) to clear the heron's head (#205). Inside, the hull's own planking is the bulwark (about 1.1 m over the waist, higher towards the ends); where its top edge is lower than a rail would be (the forecastle, the front of the quarterdeck), short rails stand on it. The deck itself stays level and reaches right out to the hull's sides, so it's wider amidships than the old square deck and narrows to the bow. Portholes are where the rooms below have them (see Below deck). The generated hull has a few holes in its planking (a slot along each side of the waist, one beside each quarterdeck stair); boards patch them from inside, shown only while the hull is (#205). The channels with the shrouds' deadeyes stand outside the hull's sides, as on a real ship, and the pin rails stand against the bulwark.
  - **The forecastle** (0.9 m up) at the bow: stairs up its middle under the **belfry**, an arch with the ship's bell (it rings; heaving to at it went in #207, and the jolly boat will take you ashore instead, #215). On it, the mooring gear: the windlass with its cable, bitts, a coiled cable, a hawse pipe, and the anchor catted outside the starboard bow.
  - **The waist** (the main deck): the main mast in its fife rail, a coil of rope by it, pin rails under the shrouds, the cargo hatch's grating, lashed crates and barrels forward; a lantern post by the starboard rail (each post's lantern hangs from its `HookPoint`, where the made post's hook is, #205); a water butt to port; the hatch below with a rail round it and a framed entrance with a lantern; the galley's skylight glowing up through its grating; supplies stacked by the cabin.
  - **The quarterdeck** (2 m up) at the stern, up stairs on both sides: its front is the captain's cabin's wall (a shut door, two glowing portholes, a noticeboard with a map and notes, a lifebuoy, an oilskin and hat on a newel), under the lantern. The stairs run between the cabin's side and a planked wall on the hull side (#205). The ship's cat is off the deck for now and will come back (#205). On top: the wheel, the binnacle's lit compass, a chart table (chart, dividers, spyglass, pipe, lamp), and the mizzen mast with its boom out over the stern. The stern lantern hangs from an iron bracket fixed to the stern's bulwark.
  - **Masts and rigging:** a rope-wrapped main mast with a fighting top, topmast and pennant; shrouds with rope-ladder ratlines and deadeyes; stays to the bowsprit; slack running rigging that sags and bows forward in the wind; loose ends and a block that swing. The course is **reefed** (half set, billowing and breathing in the gusts); the topsail and the spanker are furled.
  - **Water everywhere:** rain runs off the yard-arms, the sail's corners, the boom and the gallery, and pours out of scuppers along both sides.
  - Walkways stay open round the mast, along both waists, past the hatch, to the foot of each stair, round the windlass and round the wheel; tests check them.
  - The cabin behind the quarterdeck's door is a facade for now. The **heron captain** is at the wheel in the morning (see Conversations); the rest of the crew aren't on deck yet.
- **Below deck:**
  - **Rooms are scenes of their own (#196):** the galley (`scenes/prototype/galley.tscn`), the bunk room (`bunk_room.tscn`) and the navigation room (`navigation_room.tscn`) are each a room's furniture, walls, beams, lanterns and the collision of its walls and ceiling, placed inside the ship. It's still one ship you walk end to end, with nothing loading between rooms; a room can be rebuilt, moved or (one day, by the player) swapped without touching the rest. The hull, its outer walls, the floor and the hatch's corridor are the ship's own. **A porthole cuts its own hole:** wherever a room puts one against its wall, the hull opens behind it, ringed in brass outside, so you see the lit room from the sea and the sea from the room, and its lamplight shines out through it.
  - **The hatch stairs (#204):** from the hatch on deck, solid wooden steps run down the corridor beside the galley to the bunk room, wall to wall (the old ramp left a gap down each side). You walk on a smooth slope laid under them, so your feet never catch on a step (`Stairs`, `scripts/ship/stairs.gd`).
  - **The hull stays out of the rooms (#204):** Meshy's hull sweeps up at the stern into the bunk room (it was the "beam" across its ceiling) and pokes into the galley's corners, so it isn't drawn inside the rooms' own walls (`HullShell.clear_boxes`).
  - **The galley:** the kitchen and the mess in one room, so the crew eats at a table beside the stove. Its stations stand in a row along the bulkhead: the pantry, a counter, the chopping board on its counter, and the plate rack.
  - **The hearth:** the galley is a snug, low room (about 9 x 6.5 m, a plank ceiling 2.6 m up), closed off from the ramp's corridor and from the bunks aft, with posts down the walls under heavy beams. It's crammed with things like the reference image, but every corner is for something:
    - **The table** stands in the middle of the room under the middle beam, long side across the ship, with a bench along each side over a deep red woven rug. It's bare (#204) but for the stack of bowls you serve from: the crew's meals are what you cook. Two lanterns hang over it. A stool at its head is for whoever's in charge.
    - **The hearth** is a stone fireplace by the cooking row, its fire burning in an arched mouth (tongues of flame drawn by a shader, flickering up from the logs, #207), two hobs on iron plates set into its top (steam rises only from a pan cooking on one), a stone flue, and an iron chimney from the top of the flue up through the ceiling and the deck. It stands back against the forward bulkhead, no gap behind it. Firewood (the Meshy woodpile) with a hatchet stands beside it. Over the counters the pots and the frying pan hang from the rail by their handles, and the knives hang point down from the wall rack (Meshy, #207).
    - **By the door** to the bunks (hung open): coats on hooks, an oilskin, a sou'wester and a scarf, a lantern on a bracket, and the ship's bell: the dinner bell. Ring it (Interact) to call whoever you've cooked for to the table (#207).
    - **The navigator's corner**, by the riveted brass porthole: a pinned sea chart with dividers, a spyglass, a rolled chart and a compass.
    - **Stores:** barrels, sacks and crates, a fishing net with cork floats, a lifebuoy. (The hanging meat, the rope coil by the door and the books behind the crock shelf went in #204.)

## Islands
Each island is a verse with its own sound, palette, ingredients and people. You often hear an island through the fog before you see it.
- **Gathering:** ingredients behave like part of the island. Some need a trick, a tool or the right time: a fruit that only opens at noon, mushrooms that grow when you hum.
- **Puzzles:** OoT-style environmental puzzles, often musical. On the Bell Rocks, you ring bells in the order of your memory melody.
- **Hunting for supper (#109):** the islands' creatures are wild and fight back, and the ones you beat become supper. What you hunt ashore is what you cook aboard: a crab leaves its meat behind for the galley. Each creature has a **level** that sets how tough it is and how good its meat is.
- **People:** islanders to talk to, taste and listen to, and sometimes to invite aboard.

### The first island: the cove (#163)
A golden clearing in the fog, and the picture of what an island should feel like. Open `scenes/prototype/islands/cove.tscn` and press F6.
- **How the story gets you here (#187).** The cove is where the Prologue ends: after the lantern's soup, the deck talk and a night's sleep, a passage at sea carries the ship out of the Grave of the Lanterns into the morning, and the game comes here: the *Salt and Ember* sails in from the fog to the east, 60 m out, and slows to her anchorage during the arrival scene ("Adventure and supplies!"), which ends the Prologue. Arriving keeps the voyage, and carrying it on after that comes back here, on deck at anchor. Ashore, the first thing you find is the frying pan, in a washed-up chest a few steps along the beach from where the boat lands you. The harbour, the settlement and the old structures in the Prologue script are Chapter 1's.
- **Arriving (first sketch).** You begin on the ship's upper deck, at anchor about 70 m off the beach, looking across the water at the island and the hills behind it. The **jolly boat** sits on deck by the rail: Interact with it ("Row ashore") and the screen fades; you step out on the sand beside the same boat, pulled up at the waterline. Interact with that one ("Row back to the ship") to return. The row itself isn't shown yet, and this takes the place of the gangplank for an island with no harbour (see #67 for sailing between islands). An island is its own scene: the ship here is the real ship, standing still.
- **The first crabs.** Three level-1 crabs wait along the beach, well clear of where you land. You come ashore with your knife; the frying pan is in the chest on the beach (#187), and losing a fight brings you back to the boat on the sand. Rocks, palms and driftwood aren't solid yet (#110).
- **The lie of the land.** A cove: the waterline bites inland in the middle and comes out to a rocky point at each end. From the water: a shallow shelf of clear turquoise, a beach of pale sand about 10 m wide, a bank, then jungle, and behind it the land climbs into a hillside. Beyond that, ridges of far-off hills stand one behind another, covered in treetops and each paler than the last, so the island seems to go on. Haze builds with real distance: from the ship the whole island is a little veiled and the hills more so; the sunlit mist among the trees is only there when you're close. Out past the shelf the seabed drops away quickly into deep blue.
- **The light.** A low warm sun behind the palms, their long shadows lying across the beach, shafts of light slanting down between the trunks, and sunlit mist hanging in the jungle so the front row of trees stands dark against it. A blue sky with soft clouds. Out at sea the grey fog wall still stands behind the ship at anchor: the island is a clearing in the fog, not the end of it.
- **The water.** You see the seabed through it, wavering with lines of sunlight. Waves rise a few metres out, break white, and run up the sand trailing lace; the sand goes dark and glossy where they've just been. Rocks in the shallows are ringed with foam. Shoals of small fish cruise the drop-off and dart away if you wade close.

The player stays low-poly and softly filtered. Whether the ship's fired props should follow is undecided.
- **You can wade** in to your knees and no deeper. The island is fenced by unseen walls at its edges for now, and if you fall overboard from the ship you find yourself back on deck.
- **How it's made.** Nothing is placed by hand: the ground is built from numbers (`data/islands/cove_shape.tres`), and every palm, rock and tuft is stood by rule and by seed (the scatter nodes in the scene). Change a number in the Inspector and the island changes.

## Combat
OoT-style duels: lock on, read the enemy, strike at the opening.
- **Lock-on:** the lantern flies to the target and hovers over it, and the player strafes around it. His glow shows the fight's state: he flickers before the enemy strikes and brightens at an opening.
- **The cook's kit is the adventure kit:**
  - The **knife** strikes quickly and precisely.
  - The **frying pan** parries. Time it right for a big *clang* that staggers the enemy.
  - Later, the **ladle** sweeps, the **pepper grinder** makes creatures sneeze, and the lantern lights dark places and burns webs.

  The toolbar is the item bar, and new utensils open new parts of old islands.
- **Hearts:** the player's health.
- **Enemies have rhythms:** a readable windup, an attack, then an opening. Learning a fight is like learning a recipe.

**The first duel (greybox, #66).** Open `scenes/prototype/islands/duel_arena.tscn` and press F6: a patch of island with a landing and two crabs, until an island can be sailed to (#67).
- **Lock-on is held** (Q, middle click or the left trigger). It takes the nearest living creature within 9 m and lets go when you release it, when the creature is more than 12 m away, or when it dies. While it's held you face the creature, so walking sideways circles it; the camera swings round behind you, a little to one side so you don't hide what you're fighting, and can't be turned; and the lantern hovers over it. With nothing in reach, the button turns the camera to look the way you face. (Hold or toggle is one setting on the player's `LockOn` node, for a settings menu later.)
- **The knife is Use tool (#108).** It's found in the galley's chest (#152; the duel arena hands you one). With nothing in front of you for a tool to work on, Use tool cuts with the knife at any creature in reach, each cut's damage times the knife's own. At a chopping board it still chops. Each cut lands a moment into its swing, not as it starts.
  - **The combo:** taps chain three cuts. First a sideways **chop** (a quarter of a second, 2 m, 10 damage), then a backhand **slice** (the same), then an overhead **cleave** (2.4 m, a narrower arc, 20 damage, the longest freeze and the biggest step in). Each steps you in a little. A tap during a cut waits for it to end, so tapping in rhythm never drops one. Pause for 0.4 s and the chain starts again from the chop; after the cleave it always does. (The Quaternius animations had no thrust, so the third cut is a cleave, its heavy overhead slam.)
  - **The charged fillet:** keep holding Use tool after a cut and it charges. The knife glints, more and more, and a swish rises in pitch; at a full second it chimes, and letting go **fillets**: a full spin that hits all round (2.6 m, 20 damage) and knocks crabs back hard. Let go before the chime for a feeble spin (1.5 m, 10 damage). Charging leaves you open: you walk at half speed, can't dodge, and a blow that lands breaks the charge (so does raising the pan).
  - **The flipped crab:** a fillet that reaches a crab mid-lunge flips it onto its back. It reaches further than the lunge, so you charge, stay just out of reach, and let go as you hear the rush. So does a **perfect parry**: the pan raised no more than 0.12 s before the blow lands. On its back its legs scrabble at the air and clatter, and for 3 s every strike counts, as a **critical** for double damage.
  - Each cut is a file in `data/combat/` (`chop.tres`, `slice.tres`, `cleave.tres`, `fillet.tres`, `weak_spin.tres`), to tune in the Inspector.
- **Dodging (#108), as in OoT.** Interact, with nothing in front of you to use, jumps out of the way. Locked on, the stick picks the move against the way you face the target: left or right **sidehops** (about 2 m), back or no direction **backflips** (about 2.5 m, head over heels), and forward **rolls**. Not locked on, Interact rolls the way you're walking; standing still it does nothing. Locked on, the action prompt names the dodge. There's no moment of being untouchable: a dodge works by really getting you out of the way. You can't dodge mid-cut or while charging, and a dodge breaks off the knife's chain. Each move is a file in `data/combat/` (`sidehop.tres`, `backflip.tres`, `roll.tres`).
- **The pan is Parry** (R or the right trigger), and you must be carrying a frying pan, in any toolbar slot. It goes up for the pan's parry window (the frying pan's is 0.3 s, a setting on the pan's data, see Tools that matter); a hit that lands in that time is turned away with a clang and staggers the creature. Then it's down for 0.4 s, so mashing the button doesn't work, unless it parried, when it's ready again at once.
- **Three hearts,** in the top-left corner of the HUD. A crab's hit costs half a heart.
- **The crab** is a low-poly crab (CC0, OpenGameArt). It plays its own idle and walk, and its claws rise through its arm bones as it rears up. It notices you within 7 m. It rears up, shaking, for 0.8 s (the tell), creeping closer and turning to follow you; for the last 0.2 s it's **committed**: it stops turning and stops creeping. Then it lunges straight ahead: the hit lands as the lunge starts, on you if you're within 2 m and inside a 70° arc in front of it. Back away, sidehop or backflip out of that, and it misses. Then it sags for 1.2 s (the opening). A parried lunge staggers it for 1.5 s instead, and a perfectly parried one flips it for 3 s. Strikes only count while it's open, staggered or flipped.
- **Health, levels and damage numbers (#109).** A crab has a **level** ("Lv 1" floats over its head), and its level sets its health: 80 for each level, so a level-1 crab takes about two good openings. Each hit that counts takes its damage off and pops the number up from the crab, drifting up and fading in under a second: white for an ordinary hit, bigger and gold for a **critical** (a hit on a crab on its back does double). A hit that doesn't count shows no number, just the tink. Levels only set health and meat for now; scaling more by level comes later.
- **The kill (#109).** When its health is gone the crab dies: the longest freeze of all, its shell cracks, it rolls onto its back and its legs go still. It lies there for 4 s, then sinks into the sand and is gone. It leaves its **crab meat** on the ground where it died: Interact picks it up ("Pick up Crab Meat ★"), unless the toolbar is full. The meat's quality is the crab's level (level 1 gives ★, up to ★★★). The lantern is pleased about supper.
- **Blows land with weight (#107),** in the spirit of Hollow Knight:
  - **Hitstop.** Every blow that counts freezes the whole game for a beat: each cut its own (4 frames for the chop and slice, 7 for the cleave, 8 for the fillet, 3 for the feeble spin), 3 more for a critical, 14 for the cut that kills a crab, 12 for flipping a crab (a fillet mid-lunge or a perfect parry), 6 for a parry, 8 for a blow taken.
  - **Strikes.** A strike that counts knocks the crab back about a metre and flashes it white, and chips of shell burst off it.
  - **Parries.** A parry throws sparks off the pan.
  - **The knife and camera.** The knife leaves a pale trail through its arc, and the camera gives a small shake, biggest when you're hurt.
- **Getting hurt is unmistakable:**
  - **The moment it lands.** The game freezes, the screen flashes white, and grey fog closes in at its edges. The sound muffles for a moment.
  - **The knock.** You're thrown back away from the blow and stumble for a moment, deaf to the stick. That puts you out of knife reach, so you step back in to punish the opening.
  - **The moment after.** For a second after, you flicker and nothing can hurt you; a crab's blow in that second doesn't stagger it either.
  - **The hearts.** The heart you lost shivers with a crack across it.
  - **The last heart.** On your last heart the colour drains from the world and your heart thumps.
- **The lantern's light is the fight's state:** it flickers hard while the creature you're locked on to rears up, and burns bright and steady while it's open (staggered or flipped too).
- **Defeat:** with no hearts left, the lantern pulls you back to the island's landing with your hearts full. You keep your tools (the knife), and lose half the items you carry, rounded down, from the last toolbar slots first; a utensil counts as an item, so the pan can go. The creature that beat you forgets the strikes it took. (The arena hands the pan back if you lose it, so there's always a way to parry there.)
- **A duel can be read by ear.** The crab's claws rattle faster and faster as it rears up, and there's a rush as it lunges, so you can time a parry with your eyes shut. The knife whooshes on every swing (lower and heavier for the cleave, a cloth swish for the spins), slices when a strike counts and tinks off a crab that isn't open. A charge swishes up in pitch and chimes like glass when it's full. A dodge rustles as you go and lands with a footstep. The pan rustles as it goes up and clangs when it turns a blow away; a blow that lands thumps. A flipped crab lands on its back with a thud and its legs clatter. A kill cracks the shell (its claw clacks, deep) and thuds. Each sound is a few recordings picked at random, at a slightly varied pitch, from Kenney's CC0 Impact Sounds, RPG Audio and Interface Sounds packs (`assets/audio/combat/`). The crab's sounds come from where it stands.
- Every number above is a setting on the `Duellist`, `Dodge`, `LockOn` or `Crab` node, or in a cut's or dodge's file in `data/combat/`, to tune in the Inspector.

### Preparation
- **Dinner is the briefing:** crew gossip at the table tells you what's on tomorrow's island ("the crabs hate pepper", "the tide goes out at four bells").
- **Tonight's meal is tomorrow's blessing:** what the crew eats at dinner shapes the next day ashore (a hearty stew for an extra heart, ginger soup against the cold).
- **Packing:** going ashore is a real choice: healing buns, a jar of honey to lure something, pepper. What you take is what fits on your toolbar next to your tools, so space is the limit.
- **Taste weaknesses:** creatures react to food (pepper makes them sneeze, honey distracts, smoke makes them sleepy), so cooking knowledge is combat knowledge.

### Defeat
When you lose a fight, **the lantern pulls you out**, echoing how you met. You reappear at the island's landing with half of what you were carrying. (Later: each rescue dims him a little, and a warm meal by the stove rekindles him.)

## Player
- A young chain-tender (planned, #233; the build still has them as a castaway, rescued at sea), in a plain linen shirt, trousers and boots. On a new voyage, after you press a button on the title (her nameboard), you're asked who you are, a boy or a girl, in the title's lettering under her name; the save remembers. Their name is to be decided. (Planned: the new opening starts on home waters, so where this is asked changes; open, #232.)
- **Their body** is a Quaternius character (CC0): the Peasant outfit with a base character's head, made younger (a bigger head on a shorter body). It animates from Quaternius's Universal Animation Libraries 1 and 2: a relaxed idle, a walk that becomes a jog from 3 m/s, sword cuts for the knife's combo (each a stretch of a sword animation, sped up to fit the cut, then its recovery if nothing follows), a spinning slash for the fillet, a sword-ready stance while charging, a shield stance for the pan (they hold the knife and pan in their hands), and a hop, a jump and a roll for the dodges, with the body turned head over heels in code for the backflip. The walk and jog play as fast as the feet must go, so they never slide. You move at a gentle jog, about Breath of the Wild's pace: 3.5 m/s everywhere, aboard and ashore, so landing doesn't change how you move (it was 2 m/s aboard and 5 m/s ashore). The camera sits close, 2.2 m behind your shoulder.
- 3D movement relative to the camera, turning to face the way they walk.
- Acts on the thing in front of them: counters, the pantry, the stove, pans, the bell, the gangplank, plants, people.
- **Toolbar (like Stardew Valley):** 12 slots that hold hand tools and items together: ingredients, prepared food and utensils (chopping board, frying pan, saucepan, plate, bowl). A plate or bowl carries whatever is on it. One slot is selected at a time, and **the selected slot is what you hold**: an item there sits in your left hand (until #206 it floated over your head, a leftover from the restaurant game; with no hand to hold it, on the greybox body, it isn't shown), and Interact uses it. Each slot shows a picture of the thing's real model, taken once in an off-screen studio (`ItemIcons`, #206); a dish shows what's in it. The toolbar steps aside for any conversation or cutscene, and for an open book. Picking anything up puts it in the first free slot and selects it. The knife starts in slot 1. (Before this, the 2D prototype had a stack of 3 with a rotate button, then the 3D prototype briefly had one item in hand; both were confusing in playtests, #76.)
- **Only what you hold is used (#207).** Use works the selected slot and nothing else on the toolbar: the knife chops or swings only when it's the selected slot; holding a tomato, Use puts it down; holding nothing, Use does nothing.
- **In your hands (#207):** what you hold is shown in them and turns with you. Food sits in your left palm; a pot or pan is carried by its handle, the bowl out in front; the knife, when it's the selected slot, is in your right hand; the recipe book in your left.

### Camera
- **Free third-person camera** everywhere you walk: on deck, below deck in the galley, and on islands, orbited with the right stick or mouse. A spring arm pulls it in when a wall or the mast is in the way. It keeps its direction in the world when the ship turns, rather than turning with the deck. With a mouse, the cursor is captured while playing: Esc frees it and a click captures it again.
- **The galley is roomy enough for the free camera** (a plank ceiling 2.65 m up and the full width of the hull), so cooking uses the same camera and controls as everywhere else. Below deck, the ceilings and their beams stop the camera's spring arm, so it's pulled in under them rather than rising into the space below the deck. We tried a fixed shot there first (#63), but it looked at the player's back while they cooked at the counters.
- **Fixed, framed shots** are kept for spaces too tight for the free camera, like your bunk or an island shop, as in OoT's houses. A *camera zone* blends to its shot as you walk in, and back to the free camera's angle from before as you leave. The camera can't be turned in a fixed shot. Movement always follows the active camera, except that if the camera switches while you hold a direction, you keep walking the same way until you let go or swing the stick, so a switch never turns you round.
- **Lock-on** frames the player and the target together: the free camera swings round behind the player and a little to one side (see Combat). A fixed shot wins over it.

### Controls
Two buttons work the thing in front of you, as in Stardew Valley (#207):

- **Use** (left click) uses what you hold on it: put the tomato down, put food on the board, chop with the knife, fill the pot, set the soup on the table.
- **Interact** (right click) works the thing itself: pick it up, take food off it, open it, ring it, talk to it.

Every object follows these, so new ones need no new buttons.

**The action prompt:** the top-right corner of the HUD always says what Interact and Use tool would do right now, like OoT's A-button text: `[RMB] Take Lettuce` above `[LMB] Chop`. A line hides when its button would do nothing. On keyboard and mouse it names the mouse buttons, as in Stardew (E and F still work); after a controller press it names Xbox buttons (`[X]`, `[B]`) until the keyboard or mouse is used again. Along the bottom of the HUD are the toolbar and the name and quality of the selected item.

| Action | Keyboard / mouse | Controller (Xbox naming) | Does |
|---|---|---|---|
| Move | WASD / arrows | Left stick | Walk, relative to the camera |
| Camera | Mouse (Esc frees the cursor) | Right stick | Orbit the free camera |
| Interact | E or Space / right click | X or A | Pick up, take, open, ring, talk, gather. In a conversation: next line, or pick the reply. Chopping: stop |
| Move (in a conversation) | W and S / arrows | Left stick or D-pad | Move between replies |
| Skip a cutscene | Hold Tab | Hold Start | Held for a second, runs the cutscene to its end |
| Use | F / left click | B | Use what you hold on what you face: put it down or in, fill, set out; the knife starts chopping, and each press is a cut. Esc also stops chopping |
| Select toolbar slot | 1–9, 0, -, = / scroll | Bumpers (cycle) | Choose what you hold |
| Use (knife in hand, nothing in front) | F / left click | B | Cut with the knife: taps chain chop, slice, cleave; hold to charge and let go to fillet |
| Interact (nothing to use) | E or Space / right click | X or A | Dodge. Locked on: sidehop (left/right), backflip (back or no direction), roll (forward). Not locked on: roll the way you walk |
| Lock-on (hold) | Q / middle click | Left trigger | Face and circle the nearest creature; with none near, look the way you face |

Each action is a named action in Godot's Input Map with keyboard and controller bound, so gameplay code never checks a specific key. Panels must be usable with the D-pad. While a panel is open the mouse cursor is freed so it can be clicked, and it's captured again when the panel closes.

## Conversations
Talking is how you get to know the crew, so it comes in two kinds (the second is #94).

**A proper conversation (#93).** Stand in front of someone and Interact ("Talk to Captain"). A dark sea-teal box in a riveted brass frame opens at the bottom of the screen, the speaker's name on a little plank at its corner, and their words type out with a soft blip (lower for the gruff heron, higher for the lantern). Interact shows the rest of a line at once, then moves on. When there's a reply to pick, the choices are listed in the box with a small flame beside the one you're on: move with up and down, pick with Interact. Up to four show at once; a longer list scrolls.

**Overheard (#94).** Small pill-shaped speech bubbles float beside the crew's heads as they remark on things and chat to each other, so you overhear them as you pass. Nobody stops you or touches the camera.

- **Writing them:** chatter is in `data/dialogue/ambient.dlg`, the lantern's reactions in `moments.dlg`, in the same format as conversations. Each section is one remark; a `?` line just before it says when it can be said (`? night and raining`), and tags after its name say how often: `[weight 2]` (twice as likely), `[cooldown 300]` (seconds before it's said again, 120 if not given), `[priority 1]` (said before others that fit), `[on caught]` (a reaction to that moment). A line can end in `[thought]`, `[shout]` or `[sound]`. Nothing is said twice running.

## Cooking
Cooking is hands-on and works like a small chemistry system, in the spirit of Breath of the Wild: food reacts to where you put it. Every state food can be in is a named, designed item (tomato, sliced tomato, diced tomato, boiling water, tomato soup), so only combinations someone designed exist. Anything else simply doesn't react.

### Reactions
A **reaction** is a rule: when everything it needs is present together, for long enough, it becomes something else. Its inputs are:

- **Consumed items:** the food that gets used up.
- **Required items:** things that must be present but aren't used up. This includes the **vessel** it happens in (chopping board, frying pan, saucepan, plate or bowl), and kept food like the boiling water a potato boils in.
- **Elements:** things the surroundings provide rather than physical items (see below).
- **Amount:** how much of the driving element it takes. With no elements, the reaction is **immediate**.
- **Result:** the item that comes out, and the reaction's **quality** (see below).

**Elements** come in two kinds:
- **Instant** elements arrive in one-off steps and don't stay. Each press of Use tool with the knife adds one **cut**. Cuts chain through cut levels: lettuce → chopped lettuce (3 cuts); tomato → sliced tomato (2 cuts) → diced tomato (3 more). You choose where to stop.
- **Timed** elements stay while their source does and count seconds. A lit stove provides **fire** to the pan on it, so any heat source (the galley stove now, a campfire ashore later) works for any fire recipe. Turning the heat off pauses reactions without losing progress.

A vessel keeps checking its contents. **Every reaction whose items are all present runs**, each with its own progress, moving forward while its elements are present, so a potato and a carrot can boil side by side in the same water. Consumed food is claimed by its reaction; required items can be shared. If two reactions want the same food, the one that uses more items wins, even taking over from a smaller reaction that already started. Taking food out of a vessel cancels its reactions, losing their progress. When a reaction finishes, its consumed food is swapped for the result, which may let the next reaction start.

**Building up beats dumping in.** Because each step makes a new intermediate item, the order you add things matters without the game tracking it. A sauce built up step by step (oil, then garlic, then tomato) goes through the proper reactions. Dumping everything in at once triggers a separate, designed **shortcut reaction** that gives the same dish at a lower quality. Cooking never fails outright and never burns.

### Quality: how well you make each step (#152)
- Each piece of food has a quality from ★ to ★★★. Gathered ingredients and water are ★, the normal quality.
- **A step starts from its ingredients' stars** (their average, rounded to the nearest; a half rounds up), and **how well it was made nudges it**: made at its best adds a star, made well keeps it, made badly or by a shortcut takes one away. It never goes below ★ or above ★★★.
- **How well a step is made:**
  - **Cuts:** each cut is timed on the chop meter (below): perfect, good or ragged. A cut step is as good as its cuts on average, rounded to the nearest.
  - **Frying and boiling a dish:** caught at its golden moment is at its best; left to be merely done, it's made well.
  - **Anything else** (assembling a salad, boiling water) is made at its best when done the proper way, and badly as a shortcut.
- So **three careful steps take a ★ ingredient to a ★★★ dish**, and every press counts. Quality only ever adds. What it adds now there are no tips is still open: most likely a stronger blessing and a happier crew.

Worked example: a ★ herring filleted with perfect cuts is a ★★ fillet; fried and caught at its golden moment, a ★★★ Pan-fried Herring; left in the pan past it, ★★. A ★ tomato sliced with good cuts stays ★. Chopped lettuce and sliced tomato (both ★★), assembled on a plate, make a ★★★ Garden Salad; whole lettuce and tomato (the shortcut) make a ★ one.

(This replaced the first rule, where a step's quality was its ingredients averaged with the method and rounded up: with ★ ingredients a perfect cut came out the same as a good one, and catching the golden moment made no difference.)

### The chop meter (#152)
**Chopping is something you start (#207).** Knife in hand (the selected slot), facing a board with food to cut, Use starts it: you stand at the board, leaning in over it in a chopping stance (a Meshy text-to-motion clip, `Chop`, on the hero's rig: head down, the left hand steadying the food, small cuts going on; each cut you make still strikes down onto the board), the camera leans in over your shoulder, and a **chop meter** shows, held still below the middle of the screen (it used to sit over the board and shook as the ship rocked it), like Stardew Valley's fishing bar: a small brass-rimmed bar with a **sweet spot** in its middle (bright gold for a perfect cut, paler either side for a good one) and a white **marker sweeping** back and forth. Each Use is a cut, graded by where the marker is: **perfect**, **good** or **ragged** (anywhere else). A miss never fails: the cut still happens, just badly. Pips under the bar count the cuts made and the cuts this step takes (slicing a tomato takes 2). Interact ("Stop chopping") or Esc stops; so does putting the knife away, and it stops by itself after the last cut there is. Facing food to cut without the knife in hand, the bar says "Take out your knife". Interact ("Stop chopping") or Esc stops; so does putting the knife away, and it stops by itself after the last cut there is. Facing food to cut without the knife in hand, the bar says "Take out your knife". Interact ("Stop chopping") or Esc stops; so does putting the knife away, and it stops by itself after the last cut there is. Facing food to cut without the knife in hand, the bar says "Take out your knife".

- **It feels like a blow in the duel:** from the first cut you step up to the board and the camera leans in over your shoulder (back as soon as you walk away). Your knife hand lifts and strikes down onto the food; as it lands, a perfect cut freezes the game a beat (hitstop) with a bright slice, a knock on the board and a glassy chime, a spray of juice in the food's colour, gold glints and "Perfect!"; a good one a shorter freeze and "Good"; a ragged one a dull low knock and "Ragged". The meter's rim flashes with each cut.
- **Without a knife** the board says "You need a knife".
- The knife's sweet spot is a setting on the knife (see Tools that matter).

### The golden moment (#152)
When something proper finishes cooking over the heat (frying a fillet or searing crab, a Tomato Soup's last boil, the chilli boiled into a Spicy Tomato Soup), it's at its best for a moment: the **golden moment**. The food glows gold, pulsing faster as the moment runs out, with a glint and a chime, and its bubble says "Now!". **Take it off the heat inside the window** (take the food out, lift the pan off the hob, or turn the heat off) and it's caught at its best, with a sparkle and a brighter chime; leave it and it's simply done, a star short. Nothing ever burns. The window is the pan's (1.5 s for the frying pan), and only counts down while the heat's on.

**The pan's bubble.** Over a pan that's cooking floats a bubble in the crew's speech bubbles' style (it faces you, shrinks with distance and fades far away): a small ring filling round a flame as the food cooks, gold and pulsing with "Now!" at the golden moment, and then the dish and its stars for a few seconds ("Pan-fried Herring ★★★", gold if caught). Steam rises from the pan, thickening as it cooks, and it will sizzle, rising in pitch (the recording is still to come).

### Tools that matter (#152)
Tools are found, not bought, and a better one is more forgiving, at the board and in a fight:
- **A knife** has a **sweet spot** (how much of the chop meter is perfect, and how much at least good) and a **damage** multiplier for its cuts in a duel. The **Cheap Chef's Knife**: a narrow sweet spot (12% perfect, 34% good) and ×1 damage.
- **A pan** has a **golden window** (how long its golden moment lasts) and its **parry** windows (how long it's up, and how soon a blow must land for a perfect parry). The frying pan: 1.5 s, 0.3 s and 0.12 s.
- Better tools come later, three ways: as treasure in chests ashore, as gifts from the crew, and kept sharp (fights dull a blade, the whetstone brings it back).

**The knife in the galley chest.** You start the voyage with no knife. (Since #207 every treasure held up stands on end and turns slowly on the spot, as in Zelda: the knife point up and hand-sized, the pot and pan handle up, the book upright.) - **Counter:** holds one item, food or a utensil.
- **Chopping board:** holds one food item. Use puts the food you hold on it; with the knife in hand, Use starts chopping (see The chop meter). Interact takes the food off (losing progress towards the next cut level), and only an empty board is picked up itself. Holding a plate or bowl, Use slides the board's food onto it, if it fits.
- **Plate and bowl:** hold up to 3 food items, and assemble what's on them instantly when it matches a reaction (chopped lettuce + sliced tomato on a plate → Garden Salad). Food goes on while the plate sits on a counter. Interact always takes the plate whole, with its food; Use puts what you hold on it. A rack gives clean ones without limit.
- **Vessels don't nest:** only food goes into a board, plate, bowl or pan, never another utensil.
- **Cooking pot (the saucepan) and frying pan:** hold up to 3 food items, and cook on the galley stove. The captain gives you the pot (#186); the frying pan is found ashore (#187). Use puts what you're holding into the pan if it fits. Finished food waits in the pan. A bubble over the pan shows how it's cooking (see The golden moment). The frying pan's hob is at the front of the hearth, clear of the flue.
- **The water butt** on deck (#207): holding the pot, Use dips it in: you stoop to it, the water splashes, and the pot comes up with one lot of rainwater. A pot only ever holds one lot (boiling water counts), and with nothing but water in it, Use at the butt pours it back. Water and soup are drawn as a glossy, rippling surface filling the pot or bowl (`shaders/liquid_surface.gdshader`), not as a piece of food. The dip and pour sounds are gitignored recordings still to come (`assets/third_party/audio/water_dip.ogg`, `water_pour.ogg`); until then only the splash shows.
- **A pot shows what it's becoming (#207).** The look comes from the reaction system (`VesselLook`): as soon as everything a liquid dish needs is in the pot (water, diced tomato, chopped onion), its reaction is matched and the water is tinted a little towards the result (tomato water), even off the heat. On the fire, as the reaction progresses, the liquid blends the rest of the way to the result's colour and the food it's using shrinks and sinks into it, until the soup replaces it. Food sits on the pot's floor, or down in the water. Any reaction with a liquid result looks after itself (water boiling, the spicy soup), from the recipe data alone.
- **Once it's in the soup, it's in (#207).** Nothing comes back out of a pot with water or soup in it by hand: Interact picks up the whole pot. Ladle it into a bowl, or pour water back at the butt.
- **Getting food out of a pan:** Interact takes the top solid item. Bring a plate or bowl and the pan's whole contents, liquids and solids, are tipped into it.
- The intended fun is juggling: the stove cooks on its own while you chop, plate and serve.

## Storage
- The galley **pantry** (and later the hold) stores ingredients. Containers have a fixed number of slots, and each item uses one.
- Use on a container while holding food it accepts (and there's room) puts it away. Interact opens a small panel showing its contents, and picking an item puts it on your toolbar if there's a free slot. The pantry's panel is a wooden cupboard: a weathered wood back, each row a plank shelf, and its name on a paper label (#207).
- Browsing a container's panel pauses the game (the ship stops and cooking waits), like OoT's item screen. Esc or Close shuts it.
- The galley pantry holds 16 pieces and starts stocked with 3 lettuce, 3 tomatoes, a crab's meat, 2 onions and 2 chillies (#154) for now. Chillies and onions keep in the cupboard; chopped, in the fridge.
- Containers hold any **solid** food, raw or prepared, so you can prep ahead. Liquids can't be stored yet.
- Whether chilled and dry storage stay separate aboard a ship (an ice box?) is still open.

## Music
- The user composes the score.
- **A mix:** a score underneath (themes for the galley, the deck, the open sea and each island, shifting with time and weather), with music from inside the world woven in. The crew play at dinner, islanders sing, and the player hums.
- **The memory melody:** a fragment of a song from home, a few notes at first. (First sketch: the lantern hums three notes, A, C, B, after supper, synthesised in `scripts/companion/hum.gd`; more notes can join `MELODY` as the voyage goes on.) The player can hum it (the lantern accompanies them), and they learn more of it on the voyage. The full theme is only heard when it's complete.

## Look and feel
The game uses warm lighting, painted environments and stylised character shading. See [Look and feel](look.md) for the rendering setup.
- **Lanterns (#199):** every lantern, and the lantern spirit, is clear glass with a real flame burning inside it, not fire painted on the panes. `lantern_flame.gdshader` goes on a glass box inside the lantern and raymarches a 3D teardrop flame on a wick, painted in soft nested bands (white-gold heart, gold, orange, red tips), so it hangs in the middle and slides behind the panes as you walk round. The glass is faintly tinted, brighter edge-on, with painted shine streaks, an inner glow, a little soot and an optional iron grille in diamonds (near and far panes). The Meshy lanterns keep their frames: `PaintedGlow` with a `glass` cuts away everything of the model inside the glass box except the corner posts (`glass_cutout.gdshader`), and the frame then casts shadows, so the light pours out through the panes. Unlit, a lantern shows empty glass and a dark wick.
- **Lighting:** modern, where the cosiness comes from. Warm lantern point lights with soft shadows, glowing emissive flames, a stove fire that lights the room.
  - **Pools, not a wash:** indoors, light comes only from what's in the room (lanterns, the hearth, the porthole), so it gathers in warm pools with dark corners between. A room's own dim, warm ambient light (an interior ReflectionProbe) stands in for the night sky's, and subtle SSAO darkens creases and the space under furniture.
  - **Warm inside, cold outside:** the only cold colour in the galley is the blue moonlight through the porthole.
  - **A shadow budget:** point-light shadows are costly, so in a room only the lights that matter most cast them (in the galley, the hearth and the two lanterns over the table).
- **Particles:** steam from pots, drifting embers, chimney smoke, fireflies.
- **Small motion everywhere:** flickering firelight, swinging lanterns, a swaying net and bell, mugs rocking with the sea, candle flames wavering, hammocks rolling, curtains and blanket ends swaying, socks stirring in a stove's warm air. Slow and small, so nothing on screen lurches.
- **Placeholder cloth and paper (#135):** rugs, the runner, quilts, blankets, bed linen, ticking, curtains, the sea chart, recipe card, drawing, jar labels and painted wood are painted soft from their pixel-art originals: the same designs and colours, drawn with smooth shapes and anti-aliased edges at several times the size (gingham woven in three soft tones, the braided rug a smooth oval of rings). The bunks' and hammocks' pillows are KayKit's; KayKit's rugs were too plain, so ours stay. The open book you read in (#145) is painted the same way, from a 100 x 58 original.
- **Third-party models** dress the greybox with imported props, food and furniture. Gameplay lives on our own shapes so the game works without the packs.
- **Third-party characters** include the Quaternius player fallback and the rigged player, crew and crab models. Their animations use their original rigs.
- **The crew, made with Meshy (#139):** the heron captain is a model generated for us with Meshy (a paid AI 3D service): a grey heron in a yellow oilskin coat with toggles and a turned-up collar, a navy captain's cap with an anchor badge, feather "hands" out of the sleeves and long yellow legs, about 2.3 m tall (half again the player's height). It was made from a concept picture (a three-quarter view, so his long beak shows), rigged by Meshy and animated from Meshy's library. He's gitignored like the packs, so the greybox heron stands in without him. How to make the next one: `docs/meshy.md`.
- **Sea and sky:** fog, good-looking water, weather.

### The rainy night (the opening's atmosphere)
The game opens at night in steady rain, and Studio Ghibli's rain is the reference: patient, heavy rain that settles in, where the sound is half the picture.
- **The fog wall:** visibility ends about 28 m out at night (55 m in the misty dawn), and the fog covers the sky too, so the sea never shows an edge (#207). There's no horizon, so the ship feels like a lit room floating in grey. The rain falls round whichever camera is drawing, so a cutscene's shot out over the water is rained on too. The fog starts about 10 m from the camera, so rooms stay clear.
- **The only warmth** is the ship's: lanterns on posts and hooks round the deck, the cabin's and the gallery's windows, the galley skylight, and the hearth below. Everything else is cold blue: the light gathers in warm **pools** with dark deck between them, each lantern glowing in its own halo of mist and rain (volumetric fog), and the colour grade keeps shadows teal and highlights amber. Only three deck lanterns cast shadows (the mast, the hatch and the stern). Lanterns flicker a little.
- **The wet deck reflects:** soaked planks are a little glossy and water stands in their seams and low spots, so the lanterns streak and mirror across them (screen-space reflections). The boards vary in width, with nail heads over each beam.
- **Wet, not shiny:** soaked wood goes darker and richer rather than glossy. Water pools in low spots and plank seams, and those puddles are what mirror the lanterns and the moon. Rain rings ripple across them, water runs down the rails and mast, and drips fall from the hatch rim and the sail. Undersides stay dry.
- **Rain lands everywhere:** every drop that hits the ship splashes where it lands, on the deck, the rails or your head, and the deck above keeps the galley dry.
- **The moon** shows faintly through drifting cloud, lighting the cloud edges silver, so the moonlight has a source.
- **Distant lightning:** while it rains hard, every so often the sky flashes, a quick double flicker. On deck the whole ship is lit cold-white for a moment, every rope thrown in sharp shadow. Below deck you see it as the porthole lighting up and a moment of cold light across the galley. (Thunder to follow, with audio.)
- **Open sea (#177):** between islands there's nothing on the horizon but sea and fog, all round. (A far light in the fog, a lighthouse island seen at dawn and a near greybox island with a jetty were tried and taken out: they weren't adding much. Islands come back when the ship can sail to one, #67.)
- **The galley chimney** smokes up on deck, the plume leaning astern in the wind.
- **The sea:** a long, heavy swell with a steep wind sea and a fine chop on top, modern water (Gerstner waves, Fresnel sky reflection, light through the crests, whitecaps on crests, foam along the hull and in the wake, rain rings). Under way, the bow throws up a wave of foam and every few seconds a burst of spray. Where the water is stirred (along the hull, in the wake) tiny specks of plankton glint blue-green. The ship stays level and still; the sea rises and falls around the hull instead.
- **Sound:** rain loud on deck, muffled and quieter below deck, with waves underneath, gliding as you go through the hatch. The ship makes its own sounds from where they happen: timbers creaking at the mast and the hull, the rigging groaning, the sail cracking in a gust, rain drumming on the canvas, the bell knocking in the swell. (Each needs a recording in `assets/third_party/audio/ship/`; without one it stays silent.)
- **Dawn (first sketch):** sleep in your bunk (Interact at it; not while the lantern's low: his soup comes first, #207) and the screen fades to black: "Morning". You wake to a misty, grey-rose dawn: the rain eases to a drizzle (so the lightning stops), the fog thins and warms, the moon becomes a low warm sun, the sea stops glowing and mirrors the paler sky. Sleep again ("Rest until nightfall") for the night and the storm. Each morning is a new day ("Day 2. Morning"). One number blends it all (`TimeOfDay.dawn`), so dusk and a full day can come later by moving it slowly.
- **The moment to aim for:** sitting by the stove while the crew drift in from the rain, with stories and music. In the score, the music *is* the crew playing.
- Rain and weather are one intensity, so a heavier or lighter night changes the rain, the rings on the sea, how wet everything looks and the sound together.
- High resolution: no pixelated low-resolution viewport. This needs the Forward+ renderer.
- Placeholder art is simple coloured shapes (greybox) until real models are made.

Then **the Prologue** proper, *The Grave of the Lanterns* (`docs/prologue.md`), which has the full scene-by-scene words and carries on aboard. What's built so far is described under "How it begins". Changed: her nameboard as the title, the rail talk and the waking aboard (see below).

**The ship is the Salt and Ember.** That's her name in the story as well as the game's. Salt is the sea, and you, soaked through. Ember is the lantern. She's named for the first time when she finds you (#231).

### The Unmooring (planned, #227 to #232)
*Planned except the rowing and home waters, which are built as greyboxes. Greybox shapes first, and the lines are first drafts; each beat is its own issue, and the script is in `docs/prologue.md`. Concept pictures for the opening are in `docs/concept/opening/` (#227).*

You play the whole of it in the rowboat or on the buoy, and never walk ashore.
1. **Home waters at dusk (#227, built as a greybox).** Your island seen from the water: its lights, bells and chimney smoke. The rowboat at the jetty, the lantern on the pole at its bow. Your older sibling calls from a lit window ("Back by supper. I'm doing the fish stew."); you never see them. The marker buoy out in the dusk and the great chain rising from the sea to the island. You row out to it while the lantern chatters: you've done this a hundred times.
2. **Tending the buoy (#228, planned).** You're the chain-tender: tie up, climb onto the buoy, put a hand on the chain and feel its slow hum. The buoy's lamp is out, so the lantern gives it a little of his flame ("Just a little. Doesn't hurt."). That sets up that his light can be given away, which the ship's stove pays off later.
3. **The chain breaks and you're swept away (#229, planned).** One beat of about a minute. The chain's hum stops, a ringing comes up it, and it falls slack and slides into the sea. Alarm bells at home; the island's lights turn away into the fog. "Row! Row!": you row after it and at first it answers, but the current is already taking you. The steering fades over about 20 seconds (the rowing greybox's `takeover`), the island slides off to one side, the bells fade. "It's not us. It's the sea." The fog closes and home is gone. **It must never feel like you failed to catch it:** the sea was taking you the whole time.
4. **Into the Grave, the capsize and the rescue (#230, planned).** Swept through the fog into the Grave of the Lanterns. Lights below; the lantern leans out, thrilled ("There you are...") because he thinks it's home. They're dead lanterns, his own kind, and nobody ever told him where lanterns go ("...Oh."). The ringing, the pull, the capsize: the Grave pulls him off the pole, you reach for him and go in after him. Then the rescue as built (`underwater.tscn`) with his lines rewritten for a friend rather than a stranger. This replaces the rail scene.
5. **The ship finds you (#231, planned).** After the white you surface clinging to your upturned rowboat, half its name still on the bow (the first clue to home), the lantern in your arms, barely lit. A bell in the fog. A ship comes alongside, dark, nobody calling out. Lightning shows her name on her stern: SALT AND EMBER, the title drop (the nameboard is reused). A rope ladder is already trailing in the water. You climb up together, carrying him, and the abandoned ship begins.
6. **The title (#232, planned).** A new voyage opens on home waters at dusk instead of her nameboard. A saved voyage still opens on the nameboard, since you live aboard by then. Continue and Start a new voyage keep working (#151). Where "who are you" is asked is open.

**What's built (#226, PR #236): the rowing greybox.** Open `scenes/prototype/intro/rowing_greybox.tscn` and press F6.
- **The boat.** The player rows a rowboat (`Rowboat`, `scripts/sea/rowboat.gd`), moved by script rather than physics, so it goes where you steer and can't capsize. It rides the sea's waves: the waves are sampled at the bow, the stern and both sides, so it pitches and rolls with the swell. A stroke surges the boat on, then it glides; cruising speed is about 3.1 m/s.
- **The current.** A `SeaCurrent` (`scripts/sea/sea_current.gd`) has a `takeover` from 0 to 1 that gradually takes the steering away. At full takeover your strokes do nothing, the bow swings round with the water, and the current runs at 4 m/s. In the greybox, C sweeps it up over 15 seconds and X releases it. (The opening itself takes about 20 seconds, #229.)
- **The camera.** A chase camera (`FollowCamera`, `scripts/camera/follow_camera.gd`) follows the boat. You can turn it with the mouse or the right stick, and it eases back behind the boat.
- **Not built:** home, the buoy, the chain, the lantern on the bow pole, the sibling's call, the Grave, the capsize, the ship, any story or sound. The boat and its oars are greybox shapes until the Meshy rowboat (#234) replaces them.

**What's built (#227): home waters at dusk, a greybox.** Open `scenes/prototype/intro/home_waters.tscn` and press F6. Drawn from the concept pictures in `docs/concept/opening/` (the island from the water, the jetty, the buoy and the chain).
- **The island.** A small hill with six cottages, warm lit windows and chimney smoke, lamp posts along the jetty and up the path, and a short jetty with a big lit window beside it. The great chain runs from an iron mooring on the summit, out over the water, down into the sea by the marker buoy; its links are bigger than a cottage. The buoy is a rusted lattice tower with a railing and a caged lamp on a round float; its lamp is out (the lantern lights it in #228).
- **The look.** A violet sky with a low orange sun beyond the buoy, warm amber windows and lamps, and violet fog lying low on the water: lighting and environment only, on the existing dusk sea.
- **How it plays.** You start tied up at the jetty, facing the buoy with the island behind you, and your sibling's line comes once from the lit window (a speech bubble; nobody is drawn there). Row out (about 155 m, under a minute) while the lantern on the bow pole chatters. Some of his lines come with distance (a fifth of the way, halfway, nearly there), others at landmarks (leaving the jetty, passing the chain, seeing the buoy clearly). Each is said once, in the order it came, and never over another line. Coming alongside the buoy ends the beat (`reached_buoy`) with "To be continued" until #228.
- **Soft edges.** An invisible wall round the island stops you landing, and others stop you rowing through the buoy or the chain's foot. Row far out and the fog closes in, and after a while the lantern nudges you back ("Wrong way, chain-tender.").
- **Sound.** The island's bells ring softly every so often (a CC0 recording of village church bells, `assets/audio/intro/`).
- **How it's made.** `HomeWaters` (`scripts/intro/home_waters.gd`) runs the beat; the lantern's lines are in `data/dialogue/home_waters.dlg`, one section per moment; the walls are `KeepClear` markers (`scripts/sea/keep_clear.gd`); the chain is the Grave's `HangingChain`. The lantern on the bow pole is the greybox lantern body with a `Speaker` for his voice, not the full `LanternSpirit`, which is built to float at a walking player's shoulder.
- **Not built:** real models (the island and buoy are greybox shapes), the lantern's face (`lantern-faces.webp` is a reference for later), and the title opening here (#232). The lines are first drafts.

**Weather (planned, #239).** Being swept away should feel like the sea taking over, so wind, rain, spray and swell are to rise with `takeover` rather than a timer, with the swell steepening first (the calm dusk swell, `data/sea/dusk_swell.tres`, blended towards a rougher one). The landmarks must stay readable: in play-testing the posts were the only clue the boat was being carried, so keep the near field clear of thick fog or give things to drift past. **Open:** does the storm break the chain, so the weather builds *before* the current takes the boat, or does the weather rise with the current? This has to be settled before #239 is built.

**The rowboat (planned, #234).** A Meshy model of your home rowboat, smaller and plainer than the ship's jolly boat, with a pole at the bow for the lantern and a chain-tender's gear (a boathook, a lamp-oil can, a coil of line). The oars are separate so they swing, and her name on the bow is a board of ours so we control the letters. About 48 credits; the pictures are shown before the models are made.

### The Prologue's beats, as first drafted

### The rescue in full
Built (#119, #120, rebuilt in #183): the descent, the reach, the light going into you, the dead lanterns in the deep flickering, the thing beneath the chains in the lightning, and the burst to white.

**The dead lanterns.** All round you, hanging in the deep at every depth, tilted, still sinking very slowly, smaller and fainter until the murk takes them: some broken, some caught in enormous chains, a few with a faint dying glow. There is no seabed to see, and there mustn't be: the water should feel bottomless. Nobody explains them. They're why there's an empty hook at the stern afterwards.

**The thing below.** As his light pours into you, something enormous shifts in the dark beneath the chains. It's never lit and never in the foreground: a vast silhouette for players who look, no eye, no face, nothing that announces it. The lanterns nearest it rock in its wake, and the rumble is mostly felt.

**The light going into you.** The one touch is the whole rescue: your hand closes, his flame flares once, and then gold runs out of him along your arm until you glow and he's a guttering spark. He tries to pull back and the light holds you both. Then he chooses: the last of it goes, the screen fills with gold and goes white, and there's a held silence before the bunk room.

**How it moves.** Weight, effort and stillness, at a smooth frame rate.
- His flame is his hair: it streams back as he dives, ruffles when he's flustered, and shrinks and cools as he gives his light away.
- Effort shows: he leans his whole body into trying to lift you, and trembles at the end of it.
- You act once: your hand opens slowly before it closes on his. Until then you hang slumped.
- The flare breathes in first, and after the white there's rest.

**Left for later.** The hum that becomes the home song, which waits for music; the sea glowing back at him as he passes.

### The lantern's face
**His body (#214)** is the Meshy lantern: a dark iron frame with brass corners, cap and ring, drawn as made. Its painted glass is cut away (`PaintedGlow`, as on the ship's lanterns in #199), so his own glowing glass, flame and eyes show through, and his door is still ours, in brass, so it opens towards you underwater and his hand comes out of it.

He carries most of the opening without a word, so he needs to be readable. He has three voices. (Built in #114. A script directs him with `@look someone word`: for his eyes, `calm`, `surprised`, `determined`, `worried`, `pleading`, `effort`, `tired`, `asleep` or `pleased`; for his glass, `fogged` / `clear`, `streaked` / `dry`, `cracked` / `mended`. In everyday play he looks pleased when something good happens, tired when he's dim, and worried or set when a duel calls for it; rain runs down his glass when he's out in it, and it mists for a moment when he comes in.)
- **His eyes** say what he feels right now. The two eyes he has change shape: round for surprise, slanted in for determined, slanted out for sad or worried, flat lines for tired, curves for asleep. No mouth and no brows.
- **His flame** says what he wants and how strong he is: it leans toward what he wants, shrinks back from what scares him, stands tall when he's pleased with himself, gutters when he's weak, and cools from amber toward blue as he fades.
- **His glass** says what he's been through: it fogs when he's flustered, rain runs down it like tears, and a crack appears when he's badly hurt and mends when he's fed. The crack is rare: the rescue, and perhaps one or two moments later in the story, never routine damage.

They work at different distances. Up close you read his eyes; across the deck, his flame; and when he's a spark too small to have a face, the flame is all there is and it still acts.

## Publishing builds (#143)
Anyone can download the latest build from the repo's **Releases** page: a Windows zip and a Mac zip (universal, for Intel and Apple Silicon Macs).
- **Publishing is by hand:** *Actions > Publish build > Run workflow*, on `main`. GitHub builds the game from `main` and the private art repo, checks the exported game starts without errors, and creates a release. Its notes say how to open the game on each system and list the PRs merged since the last build.
- **Each build is named by the day it was built and its commit**, e.g. `2026.11.23 (a1b2c3d)`: the release's title, its tag (`build-2026.11.23-a1b2c3d`) and the zips (`SaltAndEmber-2026.11.23-a1b2c3d-windows.zip`). The name shows small and faint in the corner of the title while it waits for a button, so a player can say which build they played. From the editor it says `dev`.
- **Builds are release exports:** no debug keys, no console window.
- **Builds open fullscreen (#196):** Godot's borderless fullscreen, which covers the screen like a window and alt-tabs at once (`display/window/size/mode.release`). Alt+Enter or F11 switches between fullscreen and a window, anywhere in the game. Run from the editor, the game opens in a window.
- **Only the art the game uses goes in**, about 140 MB of game data instead of the gigabyte of packs.
- **Neither system trusts the game yet.** Windows shows "Windows protected your PC" (*More info > Run anyway*): signing costs money. The Mac build is signed ad hoc, not by Apple, so macOS stops it the first time (*System Settings > Privacy & Security > Open Anyway*). Nobody has tried the Mac build yet.
- **A pull request that changes how builds are made does a dry run:** the same build, kept as a download on the workflow run (for a week) instead of published.
- Credits for every pack, font and sound go in `CREDITS.txt`, which ships in every zip with the fonts' licences.
- To try a build without publishing: `tools/build/export.sh` in Git Bash (needs the art and the Godot export templates). The builds land in `builds/`.

## Architecture notes
These cover the pure-logic code that carries over to 3D. The 2D world layer (player, interaction, props, customers, navigation, lighting, the tilemap level) is legacy: it is rebuilt in 3D in the Prototype tickets and then deleted.

- Definitions are `Resource` files under `res://data/`, listed in `res://data/catalog.tres` (`Catalog`):
  - `Item` (`data/items/`): anything physical. Every food state and every utensil. A vessel has a `capacity` (how much food fits; 0 for everything else) and `carried_whole` (plates and bowls yes, boards and pans no). A `storage` kind says where food is put away.
  - `Element` (`data/elements/`): cut, fire, later cold. Instant or timed.
  - `HandTool` (`data/tools/`): a toolbar tool, and the `Element` it applies (the knife applies cut). Kept apart from `Item` because tools are never put down or into anything.
  - `Reaction` (`data/reactions/`): consumed items, required items (including the vessel), elements, amount, result and quality.
  - `Person` (`data/people/`): a character with tastes. Will describe crew members.
- Definitions are shared and never change. A physical thing in the world is an `ItemInstance` (`scripts/cooking/`) pointing at its `Item` and holding its own quality. Arms, vessels and counters hold these. A vessel's instance also owns its `VesselContents`, so its food goes wherever it goes.
- `VesselContents` is the cooking logic of one vessel, with no nodes, so it's unit tested on its own. Its changes (`add_item`, `remove_item`, `add_element`, `remove_element`, `tick`) run the reaction engine inside them; its `get_` methods only read. It emits `contents_changed`, `reaction_started`, `reaction_progressed`, `reaction_cancelled` and `reaction_finished` for the world to redraw from.
- The 3D toolbar (`scripts/player/toolbar.gd`, an `Arms` whose selected item is its "top", so every arms rule works with it), the legacy 2D arms and tool belt (`arms.gd`, `tool_belt.gd`), and each container's contents are gameplay state in scripts, not in UI scripts. UI reads them and listens to signals.
- A container's contents are a `StorageContents` (`scripts/state/`, no nodes): a list of `ItemInstance`s with a capacity, the storage kind it accepts and a take-only flag. `put_away()` follows the player's rules; `add()` ignores them (deliveries); `take_into(arms, item, quality)` moves the oldest matching piece onto the arms. It emits `changed`.
- Interact and use tool are routed the same way: the player's interaction detector sends each press to the focused interactable, which hands it to its prop. Each prop applies the interact rule itself. The 3D versions (`Area3D`) keep this design (#64).
- Shared state lives in the `Game` autoload (`scripts/state/game_state.gd`). `GameClock` and `Calendar` (`scripts/time/`) are pure logic. Whether the clock-driven day stays is an open question.
- `DayLight` (`scripts/visuals/day_light.gd`) maps the time of day to colours and lamp levels. In 3D it will drive the sun, sky and lanterns.
- `DebugKeys` (`scripts/debug/`) gives testing shortcuts in debug builds only.
- The 3D prototype lives in `scenes/prototype/` until it replaces `scenes/main.tscn`.
- **Conversations** (`scripts/dialogue/`): `DialogueScript` reads a `.dlg` text file into a `DialogueTree` (steps in order, sections by name). `DialogueRunner` walks it with no nodes, so it's unit tested alone; it reads and changes a `DialogueState` (`Game.dialogue`: saved flags, plus facts the game answers on the spot, registered by `DialogueFacts`). `Conversation` is the one node that joins it to the world: it stops the player, shows lines in the `DialogueBox` (display only), reads the buttons and emits `cue` for the scene. `ShotCues` turns a `@shot` cue into `CameraRig.hold_shot`. **Cutscenes** (`scripts/cutscene/`): `Conversation.play` starts a script as a cutscene. `CueList` names every cue and how it's written, which `DialogueScript` checks as it reads. Each family of cues has a small `CueHandler` node in the scene (`ShotCues`, `FadeCues`, `WaitCues`, `ActorCues`); one that takes time calls `hold()` and later `release()`, and the runner reads no further while any hold is open. Adding a cue is one handler and one line in `CueList`. More handlers: `StageCues` (a `Stage` is a `CanvasLayer` shown over the game; the underwater one draws a scene of its own through a `SubViewport` with its own 3D world), `ClipCues` (animations in any `AnimationPlayer` in the `clips` group), `StormCues` and `SoundCues`. `FloatOnSea` (`scripts/sea/`) keeps a shot or a prop riding the waves. `Opening` plays `intro.dlg`, telling the script whether a voyage is saved (`? saved_voyage`); `CutsceneKeys` is the F8 replay. **The lantern's face** (`scripts/companion/lantern_face.gd`, the scene `lantern_body.tscn`): one body used by the companion and by every lantern in the intro. `LanternFace` eases his eyes to a look's shape and hands his flame's strength and lean, and his glass's fog, streaks and crack, to `shaders/lantern_flame.gdshader`, the same glass and flame as the ship's other lanterns (#199). `LanternSpirit` picks his everyday look; `LookCues` lets a cutscene take it over (`@look`). At the stern, `SternSwing` swings him on his hook, `CarvedName` cuts the name into a plank (its shader lowers the wood wherever a mask of the text is white, shades the walls of the cuts by where the lamp is, and hands their slope to Godot as a normal map so real lights shade them too), and `WindowDimmer` turns the lit windows off while the title is up (their glow and the colour of their panes). A `Crewmate` is anyone you can talk to: a `Person` and the name of a script, nothing about any one character. Its `CrewAnimator` (`scripts/people/crew_animator.gd`) plays its model's animations from the `Conversation`'s signals (`started` with its `partner`, `line_said`, `choices_offered`, `ended`): idle, talk (several, in turn) and listen, named on the node, so it holds nothing about any one character either.
- **The first quest** (#158, #186): `FirstQuest` (`scripts/story/`, a node in `sea.tscn`) sends the captain up to the helm (the mark `Wheel`, along new walkways up the quarterdeck stairs) when the `intro` conversation ends with the flag `lantern_low` set (by the intro script). While the flag's set and he's at the helm, every `drink_every` seconds (110) he says one of his `thirsty_lines`, walks to the water butt, drinks and walks back. Once the flag clears he stops. `LanternSpirit.is_low()` reads that flag: while it's set he's pinned at his dimmest, tired (his glass whole, #183), his flame leaning toward a node in the group `lantern_longs_for` (the galley's `SoupHob`), and he keeps his reactions to himself (`tell` and `remark` do nothing, and his `Speaker` is `muted`, which `Chatter` leaves out), though he talks when you go to him (`lantern.dlg`'s `low` section, #184). `WaterButt` sets `fetched_water` when it fills something, and plays its drumming loop if the file's there. `SupperSpot` (his table) shares only its `remedy` item while he's low; set down, the bowl stays on the table until the flag clears, and it plays `remedy_scene` at once (`data/dialogue/remedy.dlg`, which clears `lantern_low` partway through; its marks are `SupperSeat`, `SupperBowl`, `LanternRises`, `LanternCircle1` to `4` and `LanternThanks` in the ship's `Marks`), or clears the flag itself with no conversation to play it in, and emits `mended`. `LanternSpirit.rekindle_when_well` holds a dish's warmth until the flag clears.
- **The clear day and the leaping fish** (#197):
  - **The clear dial:** `TimeOfDay.clear` (0 to 1) blends on from wherever `dawn` has things to a clear day. Each value it sets has three points (night, dawn, clear), and `colour_of` / `number_of` blend through them. That covers the sky's colours and cloud, the sun, the ambient light, the fog (thinner, and kept off the sky), the rain (none), and the sea's `deep_color`, `crest_color` and reflection (turquoise).
  - **The colour grade:** the night's grade (a `GradientTexture1D` curve per colour channel, which greys and cools the picture) eases to a neutral curve, from a copy of its own colours.
  - **The cue:** `TimeCues` (in `story.tscn`) turns it with `@clear`. It isn't saved.
  - **The fish:** `LeapingFish` (`scripts/sea/`, on the sea scene's `Ship`) jumps herrings (the ingredient's Meshy model, turned to lead with its nose) in arcs beside her, with spray each way, while `leaping` is on. They jump from beyond her side, read from the hull's saved shape (its `outline`, #196), so none come up inside the wider Meshy hull. `SeaCues` answers `@leaping_fish`, and stops them when a cutscene ends.
  - **The montage:** `voyage.dlg`'s shots are the ship's `Astern`, `RiggingDrips`, `OffTheBeam`, `FishAlongside`, `AboutTheDeck`, `AtTheChart` and `TheLookout`.
- **The voyage to the cove** (#187): the conversation, the general cues, the prompt and the dialogue facts are one scene, `scenes/prototype/story.tscn`, which the sea and the cove both include as `Story` (the intro's own pieces stay in the sea), so cutscenes play in the cove too. The first meal sets `warmed_lantern`; `DeckTalk` in `ship.tscn`, a `StoryTrigger` (`scripts/story/`) at the top of the hatch ramp, plays `deck_talk.dlg` once you walk into it after that, and the scene sets `deck_talk`. With `deck_talk` and not `at_cove`, `Bedside` offers "Sleep until we make land" and plays `voyage.dlg` instead of a night, which sets `arriving` and ends with `@travel cove` (`TravelCues` changes scene when the cutscene ends). In the cove, `CoveArrival` (`scripts/islands/`) sees `arriving`: it clears it, sets `at_cove`, starts the ship `sail_from` (60 m) away, plays `arrival.dlg` while a physics tween brings her in, puts her at anchor and you at `DeckStart` when it's over or skipped, and saves. `Opening` carries a voyage saved with `at_cove` on at `island_scene` (the cove). The cove's player starts with just the knife; chests can hold an item (`TreasureChest.item`) as well as a tool, and the cove's `BeachChest` holds the frying pan (`chest.dlg`'s `frying_pan` section).
- **Crew walking** (#157, `scripts/people/`): `Walkways` (a child of the ship, so its ways sail with her) is a network of `Waypoint` markers, each listing the waypoints it links to; it finds the shortest way between two places with Godot's `AStar3D`. A `CrewWalker` (the `Walker` child of a crewmate) follows a route in the walkways' space each physics tick, turning as it goes and holding still while the player is in the way or someone is talking with them; it emits `walking_changed`, `arrived` and `walk_ended`. `CrewAnimator` plays `walk_animation` while it moves, borrowing it from another file on the same skeleton (`walk_animation_from`) when the model's own file hasn't one. `ActorCues` gives scripts `@walk` (holds until arrival) and `@send`. The ship's waypoints are under `Walkways` in `ship.tscn`; a test casts a ray along every link to keep them clear of walls and solid props.
- **Books** (#145): a `Book` (`scripts/data/`) is a title and its `BookPage`s (a heading, lines, an optional picture), either a `.tres` of its own or made as it's opened (`RecipeBook.make_book`). `BookReading` (`scripts/reading/`, no nodes) keeps which two pages lie open and turns them. `BookView` (`ui/book_view.tscn`, in the HUD's `BookLayer`) is display only: it shows the open pages on the painted spread, reads left, right, Interact and Cancel, turns off the player's controls while it's open, and measures each page with the font to pick its size. A prop finds it with `BookView.find` and calls `open(book, player)`. A `ReadableBook` (`scripts/props/`, #159) is any book in the world: it opens its exported `Book` and can set a flag when read. A book's `opens_at` is the page it falls open at (a marked page).
- **Model loading** (`scripts/art/`): `ThirdPartyModel` loads an imported model by path and emits `dressed` for systems that need to find its rig or meshes. It hides the stand-ins while the model is present. `CharacterModel` loads a whole rigged character or assembles the Quaternius parts on a shared skeleton. `PaintedLook` applies the scene filter and assigns stylised materials to rigged models.
- **Builds** (`scripts/build/`, `tools/build/`, `.github/workflows/publish.yml`): `BuildInfo` makes every name a build goes by (its label, version, tag and zips) and reads the stamped one back; `BuildTag` (`ui/`) shows it, while `WaitCues` waits for any button. `ExportList` lists the files to export: our resources and every available pack file named by path in them. `tools/build/prepare_export.gd` stamps the version and writes the list into `export_presets.cfg`; `tools/build/export.sh` imports, prepares, exports both platforms and smoke-tests the result, then puts the two files back.
- **Cooking feel (#152):**
  - **The chop meter** is `ChopMeter` (`scripts/cooking/`, pure logic: a sweeping marker, a sweet spot from the knife's `sweet_spot` and `good_spot`, `grade()`). The board's `Counter3D` ticks it while you face the board with something to cut and a knife, grades each cut and passes the grade to `VesselContents.add_element(cut, grade)`; each `ActiveReaction` keeps its `step_grades`, and `method_quality()` turns them into how well the step was made. `Reaction.result_quality(inputs, made)` applies the rule in Quality. The HUD's `ChopMeterBar` (`ui/`) draws it; `ChopFeel` (`scripts/visuals/`, a child of the counter) plays each cut: the knife shown (`Duellist.show_knife`), the arm's chop (`CharacterModel.chop_at`, an `ArmReach` on the right arm), hitstop, sounds, particles and the word (`DamageNumber.spawn_words`).
  - **The golden moment** is a `GoldenMoment` (`scripts/cooking/`) that `VesselContents` opens when a `Reaction.golden_moment` reaction finishes, counts down while the heat's on, and catches when the food's taken out or the heat's taken away (signals `golden_opened`, `golden_caught`, `golden_missed`). The window is the vessel `Item`'s `golden_window`. `PanFeel` (a child of each `Hob`) steams, glows and drives the pan's bubble: a `SpeechBubble3D` with a `CookBubble` face (`ui/`) swapped in with `use_face`.
  - **Owning tools:** a tool is owned when its conversation flag `has_<id>` is set (`HandTool.owned_flag`), so it's saved; `Player3D.give_owned_tools` puts owned tools on the toolbar, and whenever such a flag is set. The real game's player starts with none (`sea.tscn` empties `start_tools`); `player.tscn` keeps the knife for the duel arena and the tests.
  - **Treasure chests:** `TreasureChest` (`scripts/props/`, `scenes/prototype/props/treasure_chest.tscn`) plays `data/dialogue/chest.dlg` from the section named for its treasure, sets its `flag` so it stays open, and swings its lid (the Meshy model's `Lid`, or the greybox `LidHinge`) with its light (`shaders/chest_beam.gdshader`, `chest_glow.gdshader`, `sparkle.gdshader`). The cues `@open` and `@hold_up` are `ChestCues` (`scripts/cutscene/`); the arms go up with `CharacterModel.hold_up` (two `ArmReach`es that lift the collarbones too). A line written `narrator:` has no speaker.
  - **Food models:** an `Item` has a `model_path`, `model_size` and `model_tint`. `ItemModel.food` loads the model or shows its greybox cube.
  - **Tools' numbers:** `HandTool.sweet_spot`, `good_spot`, `damage`; a pan `Item`'s `golden_window`, `parry_seconds`, `perfect_parry_seconds` (the `Duellist` reads the pan you carry each time you raise it).
- **The ship's hull (#196)** (`scripts/ship/`): `HullShell` loads the Meshy hull (gitignored art; the greybox shows without it) and fits it round the ship as it loads: stretched along by a few model-to-ship pairs, lifted so its waist's top edge is just over the main deck, deeper below than above, let out wherever a room below would poke through, and built from its starboard half mirrored (the model's port side has holes). Its shader (`shaders/hull_shell.gdshader`) draws the inside with planks of our own and leaves a hole wherever a `HullOpening` is (the porthole scene carries one, which finds its depth through the hull). `tools/ship/measure_hull.gd` saves the fitted hull's shape as a `HullOutline` (`data/ship/hull_outline.tres`); `DeckEdge` builds the decks' edges, the rails and the walls along the sides from that, so walking never depends on the art. Run the tool again after changing how the hull's fitted.
- **Books on a shelf (#196):** `BookRow` (`scripts/props/`) stands a row of `ShelfBook`s on a shelf, each the one Meshy book in its own cover colour, height and thickness (`shaders/book_cover.gdshader` recolours the leather and leaves the gilt and pages); its `shuffle` number picks the row.
- **Rowing and the current (#226)** (`scripts/sea/`, `scripts/camera/`): `Rowboat` is a script-moved node (not a physics body) that rows on the stick, rides `Sea.height_at` and tilts with it; a `SeaCurrent` (a node with `takeover` from 0 to 1, and a `took_over` signal at 1) takes its steering and moves it with the water; `FollowCamera` chases it and can be turned. `RowingGreybox` is the test scene's script (C sweeps the current up, X releases it). Built as a greybox; the Unmooring (#227 to #232) builds on it.
- **Home waters (#227)** (`scripts/intro/home_waters.gd`, `scripts/sea/keep_clear.gd`): `HomeWaters` runs the opening's first beat on the rowing pieces: the sibling's call, the lantern's chatter (`data/dialogue/home_waters.dlg`, queued by distance and landmarks, each once), `KeepClear` walls round the island and buoy, fog and a nudge far out, and `reached_buoy` alongside the buoy.
